Lecture 4: Outward-oriented Growth

It is an economic development strategy where a country promotes exports 
and integrates with the global economy instead of protecting domestic industries.
- It is closely associated with the Neoclassical approach
- Opposite from Structuralist Approach


Main Idea of Outward-oriented Growth
- Produce goods for the world market
- Encourage international trade
- Compete globally
- Promote efficiency through competition


Basis of Trade – Heckscher-Ohlin (HO) Theory
Countries export goods that use their abundant production factors intensively 
and import goods that use their scarce factors
- if they have abundant resources for that goods/svc, they shd export it
- if they have limited resources for that goods/svc, they shd import it


Factor Intensity
How much of each production factor is required to produce a goods/svc
- Formula:
  - (Ls/Lu) to measure skilled labour ratio
  - (Lu/Ls) to measure unskilled labour ratio

Ex 1. 
T-shirt requires (1 skilled worker, 2 unskilled workers) = Unskilled labour-intensive
Computer Chips requires (3 skilled worker, 3 unskilled workers) = Skilled labour-intensive

Computer chips more skilled intensive than tshirt (1 > 0.5)

Ex 2.
Country A = 72m unskilled & 60 skilled (60/72 = 0.83)
Country B = 540m unskilled & 300m skilled (300/540 = 0.56)

Country A has relatively more skilled labour than country B


Factor Endowment
Refers to a country's available resources (the amt of inputs (K,L,N,H) in a country)

Ex. 
- Country A has more skilled workers = produce computer chips
- Country B has more unskilled workers = produce t shirts
- Hence Country A will import tshirt and export chips to country B 
- Hence Country B will import chips and export tshirt to country A
- Win-Win solution

But how? 
- Factor intensity of a goods/svc (need more skilled or unskilled labour?)
- Factor abundance (country have more skilled or unskilled labour?)
- Opportunity Cost (to produce that goods/svc, country can easily 
  find a skilled/unskilled labour = lower opp cost) -> Ricardo comparative advantage 
  - (lower opp cost = give up less to produce sth)

Currency Devaluation 
A deliberate reduction in the value of the domestic currency by the central bank
- make Peso becomes weaker

Ex.
Before:
1 USD = 100 Peso

After:
1 USD = 200 Peso


Foreign buyers need fewer USD to buy Colombian goods
- Exports become cheaper
- Exports increase

Currency devaluation leads to 
- increase in exchange rate (E)
- decrease in foreign price of exports
- higher export demand 
- higher export


Problems with Currency Devaluation

Inflation
- imports become more expensive
  - Imported US beef costs more after the peso weakens

Currency War
- Other countries may also weaken their currencies
- This creates:
  - retaliation
  - competitive devaluation
  - instability



EPZ 

Export Processing Zone 
- a special area in a country where foreign or local companies can produce goods mainly for export
- German auto set up factories in Thailand and export to Indonesia 

EPZ is set up to attract FDI 

Trade creation = a trade is created between 2 countries
Trade deflection = goods are routed through/produced in another country to avoid trade restrictions 
                   or take advantage of preferential trade arrangements
Trade Diversion = switch from buying from a more efficient non-member country to buying from a 
                  less efficient member country because the member gets preferential tariffs.
