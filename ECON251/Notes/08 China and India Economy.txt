Lecture 8: China and India Economy 

China and India are 2 "Asian giants" - huge land mass and huge population but their 
growth paths diverged sharply.  

Reasons of path diversion
- GDP per capita / labour productivity
- Human Capital Index (HCI)
- Tariff levels, esp on agriculture
- Economic structure (share of manufacturing vs agriculture)

HCI = How productive could this child be in the future compared with if they had the best education and health?
- High HCI = higher future productivity of the workforce
- Low HCI = low future productivity of the workforce


5 Factors of Divergence

1.  Political System, Language & the State
China's 1 party political system → centralised state planning and faster, more uniform implementation of policy.
India's multi party democratic system -> decision-making is more decentralised and contested, slowing implementation.

China's system gives the central government stronger direct control over industrialisation and production
but India's democratic, federal structure disperses power to state/local level


2. State-Owned Enterprises (SOE vs PSU)

China — State Owned Enterprises (SOEs), companies owned or controlled by the government.
- Banking, Energy, Telecommunications, Transportation

SOEs have cheap access to credit which is breeding ground for corruption

Reform direction: 
Restructuring SOEs with greater autonomy over production scale, 
and moving toward mixed ownership (opening to private, local and foreign capital)

India — Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) / Central Public Sector Undertakings (CPSUs)

PSU (Public Sector Undertaking) = a company or organization owned or controlled by the government.
CPSU (Central Public Sector Undertaking) = a PSU owned or controlled by the Central (federal) Government of India.

PSUs/CPSUs in India are similar to SOEs in China but when comparing the scale:
- Many of China's largest firms are state-owned — dominant
- Only ~58 listed SOEs — much smaller footprint


3. Capital Accumulation & Financial Markets

Sustained growth requires an established, vibrant financial market (e.g. Hong Kong, Shanghai for China).

China's high household savings rate fed capital accumulation and investment-led growth.

Harrod–Domar growth model: growth rate g = s / v, where s = savings rate and v = capital-output ratio. 
Higher domestic savings (channelled into investment) supports faster growth.


4. Rate of Industralisation 

China: a significantly larger manufacturing sector relative to agriculture 
— manufacturing share has been large while agriculture's GDP share steadily shrank 
(2004–2019 data).

India: manufacturing and agriculture are of a more similar (identical-ish) size, 
with a comparatively larger and still-growing agriculture sector.

India's Agriculture Sector Reform — "a difficult decision"
FOOD SECURITY — feeding a large, growing population takes priority over 
export-led industrialisation of this sector

Government provides large financing (subsidies, buffer-stock support) to 
expand domestic food production

Trade-off: this financing has bred complacency and lower productivity in the sector, 
rather than efficiency gains.


5. Trade Liberalisation

Trade Openness (trade as % of GDP)
Even though India joined the WTO earlier than China but China traded much higher
but now the openness gaps narrowed down 

Tariff Rates
Both China and India cut tariffs substantially since the early 1990s, 
but China's tariffs fell faster and to a lower level than India's over the same period.

Why India liberalised trade more slowly — agriculture as the sticking point
- Food-security risk
- Poor infrastructure
- Excessive regulation
- Unstable export earnings

6. FDI Inflows
Average net FDI inflow (% of GDP): China ≈ 2.82% vs India ≈ 0.9% 
— China attracted far more FDI overall.

India's FDI weakness: minimal impact from reform efforts; the democratic system 
concentrates FDI-attraction control at the central government level without 
devolving power to local/state authorities.
— leading to red tape and bureaucracy at the state level that deters investors

7. Improving Connectivity (China)
Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) — extends connectivity from within China outward 
to "all the ends of the world".

Tools of connectivity/trade liberalisation: Special Economic Zones (SEZs), 
export processing zones, port development, and container shipping

China's SEZs were established along the eastern/coastal region from 1979;
by 2014 there were 6 SEZs, 14 open coastal cities, 4 pilot free-trade areas, 
and 5 financial-reform pilot areas.

8. Financial Market & Exchange Rate (China)

The RMB was pegged to the US dollar — i.e. a fixed exchange rate system

The Rupee is not pegged to the US dollar


Path of Convergence — Can India Catch Up?
- China's government is increasingly focused on domestic issues — a widening income gap and rising youth unemployment — shifting attention toward the domestic market.
- India's government has been focusing more on the international market.
- New wave of FDI diversification:
  - MNCs (e.g. Boeing, Apple) are diversifying supply chains away from, 
    or reducing reliance on, China — a "China+1" trend that can benefit India.
  - Emergence of the digital economy as a new growth driver


The Digital Era and the ICT industry are central to this story.

Services comprise mainly IT-related services and R&D services; 
- the computer software sector grew at roughly 30% per annum.

ICT service exports (% of total service exports):
- India has consistently and substantially outpaced China 
  (e.g. India ~41–52% vs China's low single digits through the 2000s, 
  rising to low double digits by 2018) — services, especially ICT, 
  are a genuine Indian comparative advantage.

Rationale: greater global connectivity and shifting technology increase demand for services, 
making service-led growth potentially sustainable for India.
