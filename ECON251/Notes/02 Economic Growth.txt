Learning Objectives
Understand how to compute and interpret economic growth rates, explain the key models of economic growth (basic output model, Solow's model, AK model), and describe the role of economic sectors (primary, secondary, extraction) in growth and development.
Part 1: Economic Growth
Key Recap

Industry = production of goods and services, measured as GDP (or more precisely, Real GDP/RGDP)
Economic growth = the % change in RGDP over a period (usually a year, sometimes quarterly)

Computing & Interpreting Growth Rates

A high growth rate doesn't always mean a "better" economy — context matters:

0% growth could just mean the economy is at a high base already
A country with a low RGDP but 22% growth (e.g. Nepal) isn't necessarily doing "better" than a rich country with slower growth


Case examples used in class:

Singapore — one of Asia's most robust economies, yet has one of the lowest growth rates
Japan — in recession for 20+ years, yet still a top-5 world economy


Raises the question: what counts as a "decent" growth rate, and what other evidence (beyond the headline %) shows an economy is healthy?

Basic Growth Model (Cobb Douglas production function)
Y = A · F(K, L, H, N)

Y = Real GDP
A = level of technology (exogenous — not government-controlled)
K = capital (equipment, machines, plants)
L = labour
H = human capital (knowledge/experience)
N = land/natural resources

Commonly simplified using the Cobb-Douglas production function: Y = A(K)^½(L)^½

Labour productivity = Y/L

Evidence & Causes of Growth

Labour data: labour force size vs. population, employment/unemployment rate
Capital data: capital accumulation, gross fixed capital formation
In reality, K and L rarely grow at the same rate → regression analysis (linear/multiple) used to identify which input matters more (covered in Tutorial 2)

Law of Diminishing Returns

In the short run, increasing only one input (K or L) raises Y, but at a slowing rate beyond a certain point
Example: fixed K = 4, rising L → Y rises, but % change in Y and Y/L both shrink as L increases (larger labour force competing over the same fixed capital)
Proven mathematically via first-order differentiation (dY/dK falls as L rises)

Solow's Growth Model

Focuses on capital accumulation up to an ideal level; beyond that, diminishing returns kick in

Suggests poor economies (e.g. Nepal) can "catch up" to richer ones (e.g. Singapore) by acquiring more capital — moving from point B to C
Assumes the richer economy (Singapore) stays put at C
Long-run growth needs technology applied to a given K/L ratio — this is exogenous (externally driven)
Richer countries invest in R&D → technology (A) rises → they move from C to D, staying ahead even as poorer countries raise K
Government's role: promote R&D, though it's costly/risky → links to Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) as a way to fund this

The AK Model

Production function: Y = AK
Shows there is no diminishing returns in this setup (an "overcoming the odds" case)

Role of Technology

Y = A·F(K,L): raising A (technology) increases output without needing more K or L — called embodied technical progress
Simple mathematically, but hard to achieve in the real world
Types of technology: Information Technology, Transportation Technology, Manufacturing Technology (CAD, robotics, IT-managed production)
Benefits of tech revolutions often stay concentrated within certain countries → uneven global growth/development (e.g. Industrial Revolution)
Total Factor Productivity (TFP) captures output growth from sources beyond K and L; where TFP data isn't available, proxies like R&D expenditure, ICT growth, or the HCI index are used
Open debate: pursuing more technology (A) has downstream effects on K, L, and human capital (H) — the "future of work" question

Part 2: Economic Sectoral Growth
Classifying Sectors

Exercises in class: classifying goods (rice, insurance, electric cars, computer chips, oil, cotton, web hosting, natural gas, lab-grown corn) into sectors, identifying which inputs (K, L, N, H) matter most per sector, and ranking sectors by growth potential

Primary Sector

Supply increases rapidly, prices fall, and even though demand rises, total revenue can still fall (e.g. a 10% price drop with only a 2% quantity rise → revenue falls)
Characteristics: unstable supply, perishable goods, complex storage/transport needs
Implications for intra-national and international trade

Secondary (Manufacturing) Sector

The only sector that, in the long run: shows rising productivity as output expands, has inter-related branches of activity, produces capital goods used across sectors, has strong linkages, and acts as an "engine of growth"
Linkages generate externalities/spillover effects:

Backward linkages: industry → its suppliers
Forward linkages: industry → its users
Example chain: Cotton → Cloth → T-shirt (more backward linkages = more potential growth impact)


Broad pattern: primary sector activity mainly by poor developing economies; secondary/manufacturing mainly by rich developed economies (with funding)

Natural Resources / Extraction Sector

Often mistakenly classified purely as "primary," but resources like crude oil and rare earth elements differ from crops — they require extensive processing, often classified under manufacturing
Global demand for rare earths is rising; GDP contribution is often recorded separately

Dependency Theory
- Developed countries import from poor developed countries at cheap prices 
- Developed countries manufactures and export to poor developed countries at expensive prices 
- GDP per capita of the rich and the poor economies may widen as they trade with each other (exploitation)
— motivates many developing economies to pursue industrialisation (secondary sector development), which is picked up in Topic 3

Solow Model
- Capital accumulation — machines, factories, infrastructure (K)
- Population/labour growth (L)
- Technological progress — the most impt source of long-run growth in income per person

The key idea is diminishing returns to capital

For ex, if a country keeps building more machines but technology stays unchanged, 
each additional machine adds less output than the previous one.


Main conclusion:
Saving and investment can raise the level of income, 
but technological progress is necessary for sustained long-run growth in income per capita.


Domar Model 
- Investment and Growth

For the economy to maintain full employment, investment must grow at an appropriate rate

Main conclusion:
Investment is both a source of demand and a source of productive capacity, 
so investment growth is crucial for maintaining economic growth and full employment.
