MGNT110 – Lecture 7: Managing Culture, Innovation & Change

Why Combine Culture, Innovation & Change?
The three concepts are deeply interconnected. A supportive culture encourages experimentation and drives innovation, while a poor culture resists change and kills creativity. Managers must understand how all three interact.

Organisational Culture
What is it?
Schein defines culture as what a group has learned in its efforts to survive, grow, deal with its external environment, and organise itself. It shapes how people think, behave, and make sense of their work — going far deeper than slogans or mission posters. It includes basic assumptions, beliefs, and shared values that define membership and set expectations about what is normal, acceptable, and rewarded.
Why does it matter?

Influences communication, cooperation, and decision-making
Frames what behaviours are rewarded, ignored, or punished
Affects morale, belonging, and engagement
Determines whether equity, diversity, and inclusion are genuinely practised or just policy
A toxic culture can damage long-term performance even when short-term targets are met

Case — Franklin Climate Systems (Gino, 2018):
CFO Noelle Freeman discovers toxic culture (disengagement, low morale, negativity) at the Arkansas facility. CEO Cameron Koren considers layoffs; HR head Doug Lee advocates for cultural transformation instead. The key lesson: address root causes, not just symptoms. Balancing short-term financial concerns with long-term cultural transformation is essential.

LevelNameDescription1ArtefactsVisible, tangible things — furniture, signage, uniforms, office layout2Espoused ValuesStated values — mission statements, speeches, written policies, staff language3Basic AssumptionsUnconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs — the hardest to see and change
Examples of basic assumptions: "The boss should never be questioned," "Making mistakes is dangerous," "Working long hours shows commitment."

Innovation
Definition: The implementation of a new or significantly improved product, service, organisational process, or practice. It is both a process and an outcome.
The Innovation Process (non-linear):

Initiation — a problem, idea, or opportunity emerges
Development — the idea is shaped, tested, and refined
Implementation — the innovation is introduced into practice

Employee-Driven (Internal) Innovation:
Leadership roles include sponsors, mentors, critics, and leaders. Employees at all levels can generate and implement new ideas. Example: Watsons Singapore uses the KNOW app to gather real-time anonymous employee feedback.
Open Innovation (External):
Draws on knowledge from lead users, suppliers, competitors, and online communities. Example: HubSpot builds a customer community that shapes new features and product decisions.
Social Innovation:
Aims to meet social needs and create new social relationships — impact-driven rather than profit-driven. Six stages: Prompts → Proposals → Prototyping → Sustaining → Scaling → Systemic Change. Example: Singapore's CrowdTaskSG platform for citizen engagement.

Key insight: Innovation is not only top-down. Managers must build structures that let ideas flow across teams, roles, and organisational boundaries.

How Managers Kill Creativity

Being a know-it-all or micromanaging
Excessive surveillance
Turning creative staff into mere technicians
Separating decision-makers from doers
Being aloof or gatekeeping ideas
Talking without action
Being risk-averse or dismissive ("been there, done that")


Key Takeaways

Culture is embedded in deep assumptions, values, and everyday practices — not just visible symbols
Innovation spans products, processes, relationships, and ways of working
Ideas can and should come from everywhere — employees, customers, communities
Change is political and relational, not just technical
A healthy culture enables learning, inclusion, experimentation, and sustainable change
